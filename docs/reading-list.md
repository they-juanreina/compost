# Compost — A Reading List

*Curated to deepen the veins already in your shelf, organized around the four principles you said are doing real work in the project: **decay & transformation**, **situated & partial knowledge**, **many worlds**, and **collective making**. It serves three things at once — your own thinking, your writing, and the design of Compost itself.*

A note on the shape of this list: you chose to *deepen* rather than branch, so almost everything here extends a vein you already have. But you love the essay, and you want your writing fed — so wherever a vein has a genuine essayist, I've reached for them rather than the dry monograph. Entries tagged **`[essay]`** are written in, or collected as, essays; pull that thread out if you want a reading order purely for craft. **`[start]`** marks the most natural entry point in each vein.

A word on Sontag before the veins, because she sits slightly outside them and yet belongs at the front.

---

## Susan Sontag — your model, and a thematic gift

Sontag isn't grounding theory for Compost; she's the standard for *how* to write the thing you might write out of all this. But one of her books is also uncannily on-topic.

- **Susan Sontag, *Against Interpretation and Other Essays* (1966)** `[essay]` `[start]` — The title essay argues that interpretation can be a kind of violence done to the work — "the revenge of the intellect upon art." For someone building a tool whose entire job is to interpret a corpus, this is the sharpest possible whetstone: read it as the objection your own software has to answer.
- **Susan Sontag, *On Photography* (1977)** `[essay]` — On images as evidence, acquisition, and a way of refusing experience. Reads directly against the STS vein on how representations get made and trusted.
- **Susan Sontag, *Regarding the Pain of Others* (2003)** `[essay]` — On witnessing, framing, and the ethics of looking. A companion to *On Photography* and to anything you write about whose suffering a research corpus contains and how it's shown.

## Vein 1 — Science & Technology Studies, beyond *Laboratory Life*

You have Latour & Woolgar, *Give Me a Laboratory*, and *The Pasteurization of France*. These extend the spine toward how facts — and classifications — stabilize.

- **Ludwik Fleck, *Genesis and Development of a Scientific Fact* (1935; Eng. 1979)** `[start]` — The book Kuhn and Latour both descend from: facts as products of a "thought collective." The ancestor of your three-actor provenance model.
- **Thomas Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962)** — Paradigms, and why competing frames don't share a neutral measuring stick. The classic statement of the tension your *codebook multiplicity* ADR ([0001](adr/0001-codebook-multiplicity.md)) resolves structurally.
- **Bruno Latour, *Science in Action* (1987)** — How claims travel and harden into facts; the "black box." The methodological bridge between the two Latour pieces you already hold.
- **Geoffrey C. Bowker & Susan Leigh Star, *Sorting Things Out: Classification and Its Consequences* (1999, MIT Press)** `[start]` — The single most directly relevant book to Compost's data model. Classification systems as moral and political infrastructure; what categories make visible and what they bury. Read it next to your *category tier* ADR ([0002](adr/0002-category-tier.md)).
- **Susan Leigh Star, "The Ethnography of Infrastructure" (1999)** `[essay]` — Short, and a masterclass in noticing the invisible scaffolding (standards, categories, defaults) that a tool like yours both inherits and imposes.
- **Sheila Jasanoff (ed.), *States of Knowledge: The Co-Production of Science and Social Order* (2004)** — "Co-production": knowledge and social order made together. The frame for thinking about what kind of research world Compost helps produce.

## Vein 2 — Situated & partial knowledge, around Haraway

You have *Situated Knowledges*. This vein widens it toward standpoint, accountability, and the literary register Haraway herself writes in.

- **Donna Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble: Making Kin in the Chthulucene* (2016)** `[start]` — The source of "compost" itself: "we are compost, not posthuman." Read this to know what you named the project after, in full.
- **Donna Haraway, "A Cyborg Manifesto" (1985)** `[essay]` — The essay that made her, and a model of thinking-by-figure. Pairs with *Situated Knowledges* as its other half.
- **Sandra Harding, *Whose Science? Whose Knowledge?* (1991)** — "Strong objectivity": the claim that starting from situated, marginal standpoints makes science *more* objective, not less. The argument your stance-field (`inductive | deductive | in_vivo | framework`) operationalizes.
- **Patricia Hill Collins, *Black Feminist Thought* (1990)** — Standpoint epistemology from the ground; knowledge validated within and across communities. Deepens "situated & partial" past the canonical white-feminist frame.
- **Karen Barad, *Meeting the Universe Halfway* (2007)** — "Intra-action" and agential realism: the observer and observed are co-constituted, not separable. A demanding but rewarding extension of partiality into ontology.
- **Katherine McKittrick, *Dear Science and Other Stories* (2021, Duke)** `[essay]` `[start]` — Black and anticolonial methodology written *as* stories, refusing the standard apparatus (no numbered chapters; read in any order). The best contemporary model of method-as-essay — exactly your writing ambition.
- **Ursula K. Le Guin, "The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction" (1986; in *Dancing at the Edge of the World*, 1989)** `[essay]` — Story as a *bag* that gathers and holds, not a *spear* that pierces. Haraway cites it constantly; it's the four-page argument for a research practice of gathering rather than conquest. A jewel.

## Vein 3 — Pluriversal & decolonial design, extending Escobar & Costanza-Chock

You have *Designs for the Pluriverse* and *Design Justice*. This vein supplies the decolonial-method and indigenous-knowledge undergirding for "many worlds."

- **Linda Tuhiwai Smith, *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples* (1999; 2nd ed. 2012)** `[start]` — Research itself as a colonial instrument, and what it means to do it accountably. The methods companion to Escobar; speaks straight to a research tool's politics.
- **Boaventura de Sousa Santos, *Epistemologies of the South: Justice Against Epistemicide* (2014)** — "Ecology of knowledges" and the case against epistemicide. The systematic theory beneath Escobar's "world where many worlds fit."
- **Walter D. Mignolo, *The Darker Side of Western Modernity* (2011)** — Coloniality/modernity as a single package; "epistemic disobedience." The decolonial-option argument in full.
- **Tony Fry, *Design Futuring: Sustainability, Ethics and New Practice* (2009)** — The ontological-design lineage Escobar builds on: design designs the designer back. Useful for thinking about how Compost shapes the researchers who use it.
- **Robin Wall Kimmerer, *Braiding Sweetgrass* (2013)** `[essay]` — Indigenous science and reciprocity, in luminous essays. Sits at the meeting point of *many worlds*, *decay & transformation*, and the essay form — the most pleasurable book on this list.

## Vein 4 — Collective making & the sociology of knowledge, around Becker

You have *Art Worlds*. This vein extends "facts and meanings are collaborative" toward craft, interpretation, and human–machine work — the heart of an AI-assisted coding tool.

- **Howard S. Becker, *Tricks of the Trade: How to Think About Your Research While You're Doing It* (1998)** `[start]` — Becker on the actual craft of analysis — sampling, concepts, imagery, getting unstuck. The working companion to *Art Worlds*, and a quietly essayistic book about method.
- **Susan Leigh Star & James Griesemer, "Institutional Ecology, 'Translations' and Boundary Objects" (1989)** `[essay]` — Where "boundary objects" comes from: artifacts robust enough to be shared across worlds yet flexible enough to mean different things in each. A near-exact description of a codebook shared across your interpretive lenses.
- **Lucy Suchman, *Plans and Situated Actions: The Problem of Human–Machine Communication* (1987)** — revised and expanded as ***Human–Machine Reconfigurations* (2nd ed., 2007)**. The foundational critique of treating machine action as plan-following. Essential for an AI-suggestion-then-human-endorsement design; it's the theory behind your endorsement gate. *(This is the `Suchman-PlansAndSituatedActions.pdf` from your audit — the 1987 first edition; the 2007 book is the same work, deepened.)*
- **Clifford Geertz, "Thick Description: Toward an Interpretive Theory of Culture" (in *The Interpretation of Cultures*, 1973)** `[essay]` — What interpretation actually is, and why a wink is not a twitch. The text every coding decision implicitly argues with.
- **Mary Douglas, *Purity and Danger* (1966)** — Dirt as "matter out of place"; classification as the work of drawing order from anomaly. The anthropological backbone for both "compost" and the category tier — what your orphan and multi-home codes really are.
- **Michael Polanyi, *The Tacit Dimension* (1966)** — "We know more than we can tell." The limit case for any tool trying to externalize interpretive judgment into explicit codes and definitions.

## Vein 5 — The transcript itself: transcription as an interpretive act

*Added after your transcript audit. This is the one part of Compost's pipeline none of the four veins above names directly — and it's where interpretation begins, before a single code is applied. Compost transcribes with descriptive cues (typed silences, laughter, sighs, prosody) and diarizes by speaker; every one of those choices is an argument about what matters. These two are the canonical statements of that fact, from linguistic anthropology / discourse analysis. Compost now names this commitment in the codebase itself — [ADR 0006 (ingest provenance)](adr/0006-ingest-provenance.md) treats a transcript as attributed source rather than an endorsed artifact, operationalizing Bucholtz's question of who made a rendering and whether its lineage can be seen.*

- **Elinor Ochs, "Transcription as Theory" (in *Developmental Pragmatics*, eds. Ochs & Schieffelin, 1979, pp. 43–72)** `[essay]` `[start]` — The founding claim that a transcript is never neutral: layout, what's included, and which symbols you use all encode theoretical commitments. Read it straight against Compost's descriptive-cue set — each cue you choose to render (or not) is a theory of what counts as data.
- **Mary Bucholtz, "The politics of transcription" (*Journal of Pragmatics* 32:10, 2000, pp. 1439–1465)** `[essay]` — Splits the problem into the *interpretive* (what gets transcribed) and the *representational* (how it's rendered), and shows both as acts of power demanding the transcriber's visibility and accountability. Maps almost one-to-one onto your three-actor provenance and diarization: who made this rendering, and can the lineage be seen?

## A short methods bench (you already have Saldaña, DeTAILS, *AI for Qualitative Research*)

- **Virginia Braun & Victoria Clarke, *Thematic Analysis: A Practical Guide* (2022)** `[start]` — The reflexive-TA source your DeTAILS paper is built on. Read it to see what the LLM workflow is trying to preserve (and what it risks flattening).
- **Kathy Charmaz, *Constructing Grounded Theory* (2nd ed., 2014)** — Constructivist grounded theory: codes and categories as *constructed*, not discovered. The methodological match for your provenance-first stance, and a direct deepening of ADRs [0001](adr/0001-codebook-multiplicity.md) (codebook multiplicity) and [0002](adr/0002-category-tier.md) (category tier).

## If you want a reading order

For the **project**: Ochs & Bucholtz (transcription) → Bowker & Star → ADRs [0001](adr/0001-codebook-multiplicity.md) & [0002](adr/0002-category-tier.md) (codebook multiplicity, category tier) → Star & Griesemer (boundary objects) → Suchman → Charmaz. *(Ochs and Bucholtz come first because transcription is the pipeline's first interpretive move.)*
For the **writing**: Sontag, *Against Interpretation* → Le Guin, "Carrier Bag" → McKittrick → Kimmerer → Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble*.
For **range/pleasure**: Kimmerer → Sontag → Tsing's *The Mushroom at the End of the World* (2015) — not in a vein above, but the definitive book-length meditation on collaborative survival, decay, and life in ruins. The most "compost" book ever written; save it for when you want all four principles in one place.
